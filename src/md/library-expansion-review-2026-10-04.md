# Library expansion review — 4 October 2026

Reviewed local content revision `2026-10-04-v3`, commit `63c63cb`. This is a recommendation, not an implementation or deployment.

The subsequent [mastery and library plan](mastery-and-library-plan-2026-10-04.md) incorporates the user’s preference to keep existing cases at fixed levels and add three variable-level cases. It supersedes the rollout proposed below.

Recommendation: improve the meaning of the existing three difficulty levels, add cases that broaden the emotional situations available, and introduce a small number of distinct skills. A fourth level and a large increase in interchangeable examples would add less value.

## Current coverage and evidence

The playable library contains 12 skills, nine adult cases and 12 items per skill/case: 1,296 client/example pairs in English and Norwegian. There are three cases at each difficulty and 432 items per level. All 1,296 item difficulty labels match their case's fixed difficulty; none uses a different item-specific tier. The nine client ages range from 24 to 45. Case formulations repeatedly connect present problems to earlier shame, conditional worth, abandonment or trauma.

The core skills provide strong coverage of empathy, exploration, emotional contact, rationale, disclosure, chair-work recognition/setup and repair. They provide less explicit practice in responding to change, consolidating it, following a chair task beyond its first turn, and ending a session.

Empathic Refocusing has experimental descriptions and authoring guidance, but no statement bank in `STATEMENT_SETS` and no entry in the playable runtime. Promoting it needs content, translations and feedback, rather than a visibility toggle.

Evidence for this review includes the complete skill list and case backgrounds, case bibles, runtime manifest, registry difficulty distribution, feedback cues, relevant authoring instructions, previous editorial reviews and twelve selected English practice examples across four skills and three cases, used to examine challenge differences. It does not independently reassess the quality of every item or establish a clinically validated difficulty scale. The earlier editorial passes remain the record of which examples received close bilingual reading.

## 1. Difficulty: retain three levels, make them about the exercise

The present labels combine the person's overall complexity with the challenge of a particular response. For example, Marcus's exploration item 11 expresses a little sadness while hearing a song and can invite a small moment of contact. It is labeled hard because Marcus is hard. Other Marcus items ask the therapist to respond to a strong boundary or trauma cue. These are different tasks even within the same background.

Use the same person at different levels, with level defined relative to the selected skill:

| Level | Proposed exercise characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| Easy | One clear emotional cue or task marker; a focused response; the role-player can accept a fitting response. |
| Moderate | Mixed feeling, an indirect cue or a protective shift; the therapist must choose a focus and check fit. |
| Hard | Ambiguous cues, competing meanings, a correction or subtle relational strain; the therapist must adapt without defending the first response. |

These are proposed authoring anchors to calibrate through trainee and instructor review, not validated thresholds. Risk, diagnosis, length of speech and emotional intensity should not substitute for difficulty. Genuine immediate risk changes what response is needed; it should not be added merely to make an exercise harder.

Keep the full twelve-item round at the chosen level, the same roles throughout and four ratings after sets of three. Use a compact level choice during host setup. A small optional therapist challenge check at the end of a round could help choose the next level; keep it distinct from the existing 1–5 performance rating. Do not change the practiced level retrospectively because a trainee found it easy.

Pilot three complete versions of one existing case for one skill before expanding the matrix. That means 36 pairs for the pilot cell, with the existing twelve retained or reused where suitable. Blind comparison should examine whether the challenge distinctions hold in both languages. Do not triple the whole corpus immediately.

The [APA manual's resource page](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/deliberate-practice-emotion-focused-therapy) describes adjusting difficulty to the trainee's experience. It supports adjustment as a practice principle; the case-independent design above is our proposed implementation. The detailed publisher appendix was not readable through the web tool in this review.

Implementation would need to separate case identity from practice level in content selection and room state, and record the selected level in new ratings. Historical ratings must retain their original labels. The radar can keep its three colored series. This review changes none of those records.

## 2. Cases: add three distinct situations, beginning with bereavement

| Proposed case | Gap addressed | Editorial focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An older adult grieving a partner | No current case is over 45; bereavement and later-life changes have no dedicated case. | Ordinary loss, loneliness, changed routines, moments of relief or joy, and guilt about continuing life. Do not automatically formulate grief as childhood abandonment. |
| An adult adapting to chronic illness or disability | Loss of capacity, dependence, uncertain futures and being disbelieved have no dedicated case. | Fear and anger alongside dignity, autonomy and actual bodily limits. Avoid implying that physical symptoms are merely emotional or that acceptance means giving up. |
| An adult navigating discrimination and belonging | Existing case identities are not a substitute for sustained practice with social context and identity. | Specific experiences of exclusion, identity conflict and support, with room to repair a therapist's assumption. Do not reinterpret actual discrimination as a mistaken perception or assign a hard level because of identity. |

These are distinct case concepts, not diagnoses or ready-made dossiers. Give each a recognizable voice, relationships, strengths and ordinary life outside the presenting problem. Include agency, adaptive anger, relief, pride and connection across new and existing cases, so practice does not always mean eliciting more pain.

For the third case, select one concrete lived context rather than combining migration, religion, sexuality, race and disability into a generic representative person. This approach is consistent with the [APA multicultural guidelines](https://www.apa.org/about/policy/multicultural-guidelines.html), which emphasize individual identity and social context; the specific case priorities are our editorial judgment.

Avoid adding another perfectionistic professional, shame-driven angry man or abandonment-focused young woman first: those processes already have substantial coverage. Young people and couple/family work can follow as separate expansions because their context, consent, roles and training targets differ from this adult individual-therapy library.

Start each new case with complete twelve-item rounds for the skills where it adds the clearest value, then expand. The current validator requires every production skill to cover every case, so selective coverage requires an explicit coverage map and validation changes. One case across all current skills otherwise entails 144 bilingual pairs; three entail 432. Do not satisfy the matrix by writing strained chair-work or disclosure prompts merely to fill cells.

## 3. Skills: add narrowly defined targets

The app is a focused practice library, not a complete EFT training curriculum. That remains useful. The next skills should address distinct response choices rather than rename what the current twelve already teach.

| Priority | Candidate | Target and relation to existing skills |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | Consolidating emotional change | Help the client put an emerging shift into their own words, recognize its meaning and carry it forward without premature advice or declaring the problem resolved. Distinct from continually deepening pain. |
| First | Empathic refocusing | Notice a shift away from a just-emerging feeling, check its meaning and invite return when wanted. The experimental guide is a starting point. Distinct from exploring emotion that is still in contact; a tangent is not automatically avoidance. |
| Next | Closing a session after emotional work | Acknowledge time, hear what the client is taking away, attend to activation or unfinished work and agree on an appropriate stopping point. Distinct from merely grounding during intense affect. |
| Later | Experiential focusing | Work with an unclear whole felt sense and check a word or image against it. Needs a clearer target than the existing exploratory question or a routine body scan. |
| Later, with task format | Chair-work beyond setup and compassionate self-soothing | Practise selected change points in a dialogue. These need short sequences or a few exchanges, not a claim to complete an entire task in one response. |

Systematic evocative unfolding is another legitimate future task module, but broadening the task curriculum can wait until the short-response skills and progression work are established.

The [isEFT individual-training standards](https://www.iseft.org/page-18299) include focusing, unfolding, chair dialogues and self-soothing. The [EFT therapist competence framework](https://emotionfocusedtherapy.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/FC-EFT-Competence-v1.03-2.pdf), especially sections 3.1–3.4, includes refocusing, task change points, consolidation and session closing. These sources establish curricular relevance; they do not endorse this app, its example responses or the proposed priority order.

Every added skill needs a specific observer target, matching self-assessment cues, bilingual instructions and examples, and stable identifiers. Keep the current performance scale. The current validation also assumes that all production skills have a Goldman Exercises 1–12 benchmark. New extensions need correctly attributed sources and their own benchmark/review requirements; they should not be represented as additional Goldman exercises.

## Suggested sequence

1. Pilot one case at three levels for one existing skill; keep full twelve-item rounds and the four ratings.
2. Add the older bereavement case, then illness/disability and a specific discrimination/belonging case.
3. Introduce consolidating change and refocusing as distinct skill extensions; session closing follows.
4. Add selected task modules when brief multi-turn practice can preserve the task's actual purpose.

No app behavior, content banks, live accounts or ratings are modified by this review.
